# Grocery Shopping in Brussels: A Newcomer's Guide to Supermarkets & Specialty Stores

Your first grocery run in a new city is a small culture shock. The chains have unfamiliar names, the layout logic is different, loyalty cards seem to unlock half the store's prices, and then you discover almost everything shuts on Sunday afternoon. None of it is complicated once you know the map, it just isn't explained anywhere. Here's the newcomer's cheat sheet to shopping for food in Brussels, from the budget chains to the weekend markets to the specialty shops that make a foreign kitchen feel like home.

## The supermarket landscape, from cheapest to poshest

Belgium has an unusually crowded supermarket scene for a small country, and price differences between chains are real, not marketing. Roughly from budget to premium:

- **Aldi and Lidl**: the cheapest full shops, especially for staples, dairy, and their themed international weeks (keep an eye out, they rotate country specials and some become cult favourites). Smaller stores, fewer brand choices, but the savings add up fast on a weekly shop.
- **Colruyt**: Belgians' favourite for "lowest price guaranteed," genuinely competitive, with a slightly no-frills warehouse feel. You'll want a **Colruyt XTRA card** (free) to unlock extra discounts.
- **Okay and Spar**: smaller neighbourhood formats, handy for topping up rather than a full shop, usually pricier per item than the big-box stores.
- **Carrefour** (hypermarket, Express, and Market formats): the most common chain around Brussels, reasonable prices, and the Express/Market formats are everywhere for a quick stop on the way home.
- **Delhaize**: well-stocked, good produce and bakery sections, slightly more expensive overall. The small AD Delhaize convenience shops are a lifesaver for late evenings and Sundays (see below).
- **Match**: mid-range, decent quality, present in several neighbourhoods.

A practical pattern a lot of residents settle into: a weekly bulk shop at Aldi, Lidl, or Colruyt, topped up through the week at whichever Carrefour Express or Delhaize is on the walk home. If you're in a [coliving house](/actors), splitting a big Colruyt or Carrefour run between housemates and cooking a shared dinner is both cheaper and, frankly, more fun than shopping solo every time.

## Loyalty cards are not optional if you want the real price

This catches almost every newcomer: the shelf price and the loyalty-card price can be meaningfully different, sometimes 10-20% apart on promoted items. Signing up takes two minutes in-store or via the app, costs nothing, and is genuinely worth doing in your first week:

- **Colruyt XTRA**
- **Delhaize Plus**
- **Carrefour Bonus Card**

Keep the apps on your phone, most tills can scan the digital version so you don't need a physical card cluttering your wallet.

## The Sunday (and evening) closing problem

Belgium takes Sunday rest seriously, and it genuinely surprises people who move from countries with 24/7 shopping. Most full-size supermarkets close by early-to-mid afternoon on Sunday, and some close all day. If you need to shop on a Sunday or late at night, your options are:

- **Night shops ("nachtwinkels"/"night shops")**: small independent convenience stores, common in dense neighbourhoods like Ixelles, Saint-Gilles, and around Flagey, open late and on Sundays, at a modest markup.
- **AD Delhaize / Carrefour Express**: many of the small-format city-centre branches do open on Sunday mornings, worth checking the one nearest you.
- **Petrol station shops**: a real backup for basics in a pinch.

The lesson most people learn the hard way exactly once: do your big shop on Saturday, not Sunday morning, and keep a mental note of which corner shop near you is open late.

## Markets and specialty stores for the things supermarkets don't do well

Supermarkets cover the basics, but Brussels' real food culture lives in its markets and specialty shops. For fresh produce, bread, cheese, and genuinely good prices on fruit and vegetables, nothing beats the weekend markets, our full [guide to Brussels' best markets](/blog/brussels-markets-guide) covers the Sunday giant at Gare du Midi and the smaller neighbourhood ones at Flagey and Châtelain.

Beyond the markets, a few categories worth knowing:

- **Bio/organic shops** (Färm, Bioplanet, and independent biowinkels) if you want organic without hunting through a regular supermarket's limited shelf.
- **International grocers**: Matonge (near Ixelles/Saint-Gilles) for African and Caribbean ingredients, the areas around Gare du Midi and Anderlecht for Middle Eastern, Turkish, and North African spices, breads, and halal butchers, and pockets near Rue de Brabant for South Asian groceries. If a specific ingredient from home is missing from your local supermarket, there's a very good chance one of these neighbourhoods has it.
- **Boulangeries and boucheries**: independent bakeries and butchers are still a real part of daily life here, not a novelty. A decent one near your flat is worth finding early.
- **Cheese and chocolate shops**: for the full Belgian experience rather than the supermarket shelf version, worth doing at least once, see our guide to [Belgian food you must try](/blog/belgian-food-you-must-try) for what to look for.

## Budgeting for food in Brussels

Food is one of the more controllable parts of a Brussels budget, cooking at home on a Colruyt/Aldi rotation keeps costs well below eating out regularly. For how groceries fit into the bigger monthly picture, our [monthly budget guide for newcomers](/blog/monthly-budget-brussels-newcomers) breaks down typical spending by category, and the [cost of living overview](/blog/cost-of-living-brussels-2026) gives the full annual picture.

A few habits that make a real difference:

1. **Sign up for loyalty cards in week one.** The discount difference compounds over months.
2. **Shop the weekend market for produce, the supermarket for everything else.** Cheaper and better quality for fruit and veg.
3. **Buy in bulk with housemates.** Rice, pasta, oil, and cleaning products are cheaper per unit in bigger formats, and far more manageable to split in a shared house than to store solo.
4. **Watch the own-brand aisle.** Colruyt's "Boni" and Carrefour/Delhaize's own lines are often near-identical to name brands at a noticeably lower price.

## Settling into your local shopping routine

Within a few weeks, most newcomers land on a routine: a go-to supermarket for the weekly basics, a favourite market stall, and one or two specialty shops for the things that make a kitchen feel properly like home.
