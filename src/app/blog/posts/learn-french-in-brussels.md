# How to Learn French in Brussels (Free & Cheap Options)

You can survive in Brussels on English — but you'll *live* here properly once you speak some French. It unlocks daily life, friendships, dating, and admin, and locals warm to anyone who tries. The good news: you don't need to spend a fortune. Here's how to actually learn, cheaply.

## Why French first?

Brussels is officially bilingual (French and Dutch), but day-to-day life is majority French-speaking, so it's the pragmatic first choice for most newcomers. (Dutch is a valuable add-on, especially for jobs — more in our [French or Dutch guide](/blog/french-or-dutch-brussels-language).) Even a beginner level pays off socially far faster than professionally.

## Free & cheap classes

Brussels is unusually generous with subsidised language learning:

*   **Commune classes:** many Brussels communes and local non-profits (*asbl*) run **free or very cheap** French courses for residents. Ask at your commune — this is the best-kept secret for newcomers.
*   **Maison de la Francité:** a dedicated organisation for French language and culture, with resources, activities, and courses.
*   **CVO (adult education centres) & Promotion Sociale:** subsidised evening and weekend courses at low cost, widely available across the city.
*   **University language centres** (ULB, etc.) sometimes offer courses open to the public.

## Language exchanges (free + social)

The fastest, most fun way to improve — and to make friends:

*   **Tandem exchanges:** pair up with someone who wants to learn your language and trade an hour of French for an hour of English over coffee.
*   **Conversation tables / *cafés langues*:** regular meetups in bars and cafés where people practise different languages. Free, low-pressure, and social.
*   **Language apps** (Duolingo, Babbel) for vocabulary drilling — useful, but they plateau without real conversation.

## Paid (if you want structure)

If you prefer a formal, faster track:
*   **Alliance Française de Bruxelles** — the classic, structured French institution.
*   **Private language schools** and tutors (italki, Preply for online 1-on-1) — flexible but pricier.

## The etiquette that matters more than grammar

Before you're fluent, one habit transforms every interaction: **greet in French first.** A simple "Bonjour" before you ask anything — in shops, at counters, on the phone — is the difference between polite and rude in Belgium. Add "s'il vous plaît," "merci," and "bonne journée" and you've bought enormous goodwill for four words. See [how to fit in](/blog/how-to-fit-in-brussels).

And remember Belgian French has quirks: **septante** (70) and **nonante** (90), plus local expressions. Our [Brussels slang guide](/blog/brussels-slang-dictionary) gets you sounding local.

## The best free practice of all: your housemates

Here's the honest shortcut we keep coming back to. Apps and even classes plateau without daily use — and the cheapest, most consistent practice is the people you live with. In a mixed [coliving house](/actors), Belgian and Francophone housemates will happily correct your French over dinner, and the stakes ("pass the salt," "whose turn is the bins?") are low enough that you actually speak instead of freezing. A shared kitchen beats any app.

## A realistic learning plan

1.  **Week 1:** learn the greetings and survival phrases (do this before you arrive).
2.  **Month 1:** enrol in a free/cheap commune or CVO course.
3.  **Ongoing:** add a weekly conversation table or tandem for real speaking practice.
4.  **Daily:** speak French with housemates, order in French, greet everyone.

Do that, and within a few months Brussels stops being a city you *cope* in and becomes one you *belong* in.
