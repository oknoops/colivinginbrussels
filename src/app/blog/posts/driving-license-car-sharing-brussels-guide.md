# Driving in Brussels: Licenses, Car-Sharing and Parking for Newcomers

Brussels is a city that quietly discourages car ownership, and once you've lived here a few months, you'll understand why. Narrow one-way streets, famously scarce parking, a dense tram and metro network, and a region actively investing in low-traffic zones all push daily life away from the driver's seat. Still, plenty of newcomers arrive with a car already, or need to drive occasionally for work, weekend trips, or moving furniture, and the admin around that is one of those unglamorous but important things nobody explains clearly. Here's what actually matters.

## Do you need to exchange your driving license?

This depends almost entirely on where your license was issued, and it's worth sorting out early, since driving on an invalid license (even unknowingly) can void your insurance in an accident.

**EU/EEA license holders** generally don't need to exchange anything. Your license remains valid to drive in Belgium for as long as it's valid at home, though many people choose to exchange it for a Belgian one eventually simply for convenience (easier renewals, no foreign postal address issues).

**Non-EU/EEA license holders** face a more varied picture. Belgium has reciprocal exchange agreements with a number of countries (several Canadian provinces, several US states, Japan, South Korea, and others), meaning you can swap your foreign license for a Belgian one without retesting, provided you do it within the legal window after registering as a resident, typically around a year. Miss that window and you may be required to pass the Belgian theory and practical exams from scratch. If your country of issue has no exchange agreement with Belgium at all, you'll likely need to go through Belgian driving school and testing regardless of how long you've been driving elsewhere.

The practical first step is always the same: check your situation with your commune's population service when you [register as a resident](/blog/how-to-register-brussels-expat-guide), since exchange rules and required documents (often including a certified translation of your original license) are handled at that level and can shift. Don't assume last year's forum post still applies.

## Is owning a car actually worth it here?

For most people living centrally in Brussels, genuinely, no. A few honest reasons:

- **Parking is scarce and contested.** Many central neighbourhoods operate resident parking zones where non-residents pay steeply by the hour, and even residents often circle blocks hunting for a spot. If your building doesn't come with a dedicated space, budget real time and sometimes real money (private garage rental runs well into three figures per month in central communes) for parking.
- **Low Emission Zones restrict older vehicles.** Brussels' LEZ bans the most polluting diesel and petrol vehicles outright, with rules tightening over time. An older car bought cheaply abroad may simply not be allowed into the city.
- **Public transport covers the city well.** The STIB/MIVB network of metro, tram and bus reaches almost everywhere useful, and combined with cycling for shorter hops, most people find they drive less than they expected once they're settled. Our [public transport guide](/blog/brussels-public-transport-guide-2026) and [cycling guide](/blog/cycling-in-brussels-guide) cover both in detail.
- **Running costs add up fast.** Insurance, the annual road tax, technical inspection (*contrôle technique*/*technische keuring*) for older cars, and Belgium's relatively high fuel prices make occasional use expensive per trip compared with alternatives.

Where a car genuinely helps: families with young kids and a lot of gear, frequent trips outside the city (to [day trips from Brussels](/blog/brussels-day-trips-guide) in the Ardennes or the coast), or jobs requiring travel between sites not well served by transit.

## Car-sharing: the middle ground most newcomers land on

This is the option that quietly solves the problem for a lot of people, and it's worth knowing about before you default to buying.

**Free-floating car-sharing** (pick up a car anywhere in a zone via an app, drop it off anywhere in the zone when done) works well for one-off errands, an IKEA run, or getting somewhere transit doesn't reach easily late at night. You pay by the minute or by a day package, with no commitment and no parking headache of your own, since you simply leave the car in a valid spot when finished.

**Station-based car-sharing** (book a specific car at a fixed location, return it to the same spot) tends to be cheaper per hour for planned trips, like a weekend away or a predictable weekly errand, and suits people who want a car at a known time rather than on impulse.

Several operators run both models across Brussels, with fleets that include electric options exempt from LEZ restrictions. Pricing is generally membership-plus-usage, and most let you sign up with a foreign license (once it's valid to drive in Belgium per the section above) and a credit card, with no long-term contract. For most newcomers, trying car-sharing for a month or two before deciding whether to buy is the sensible order of operations, not the other way around.

## If you do bring or buy a car

A few admin basics to have on your checklist:

- **Insurance** (*assurance auto*/*autoverzekering*) is mandatory and should be arranged before the car touches the road, not after.
- **Registration** (*immatriculation*/*inschrijving*) with Belgian plates is required once you're a resident, even for a car brought from abroad.
- **Technical inspection** is required periodically for most cars past a few years old, and you'll need a valid certificate to register or re-register.
- **Parking permits** for your street, if your commune runs a resident zone, are applied for through the commune, usually tied to your registered address.

None of this is particularly hard, it's just spread across a few different counters, so budget a few separate half-days rather than expecting to sort it all in one visit, similar to the patience you'd budget for [opening a Belgian bank account](/blog/how-to-open-bank-account-brussels) or any other first-months admin task.

## The coliving angle

If you're weighing up where to live against whether you'll need a car, it's worth factoring into the decision rather than treating them separately. A [coliving house](/actors) in a well-connected neighbourhood close to a metro or tram line often removes the need for a car entirely, which is one of the quieter cost savings people underestimate when comparing coliving rent to a cheaper flat further out that would require a car to make work. Our [neighbourhood comparison guide](/neighborhoods) factors in transit links alongside rent and lifestyle, and the [coliving matchmaker](/matchmaker) can help you find a house in a spot where you genuinely won't miss having a car, or where your future housemates might already share one through a car-sharing membership.

## The short version

EU/EEA licenses stay valid as-is; non-EU licenses depend on reciprocal agreements and a registration-linked deadline, so check with your commune early. Before buying a car, seriously consider whether Brussels' public transport, cycling infrastructure and car-sharing options cover your actual needs, since for most residents living centrally, they do. And if you do need a car, budget for parking and admin as a genuine ongoing cost, not an afterthought.
