# Childcare in Brussels: Crèches, Daycare and the Waitlist Every Parent Should Know About

Ask any parent who has lived in Brussels for more than a year and childcare will come up within the first few minutes. Not because the quality is bad, most crèches and childminders here are warm, professional, and genuinely well run, but because demand for subsidised spots massively outstrips supply, and the system rewards families who understand it early. If you're expecting a child or relocating with a baby or toddler, this is one of the few areas of Brussels life where "figure it out when you arrive" can genuinely cost you a spot. Here's how it actually works.

## Two networks, two languages

Belgium's childcare system is split along linguistic lines, and Brussels, being officially bilingual, hosts both.

- **ONE (Office de la Naissance et de l'Enfance)** oversees French-speaking childcare: crèches, home-based childminders (*accueillantes*), and parenting support services.
- **Kind en Gezin** oversees the Dutch-speaking equivalent, including *kinderdagverblijven* (day nurseries) and *onthaalouders* (registered childminders).

You aren't required to pick a network based on your own languages, plenty of Dutch-speaking and international families use ONE-affiliated crèches and vice versa, but each network runs its own registration lists, subsidy rules, and search tools, so it helps to know which one you're dealing with before you start applying.

## Subsidised vs. private: the real difference is cost, not quality

Broadly, childcare in Brussels falls into two categories:

- **Subsidised ("conventionné") crèches** charge fees on a sliding scale tied to household income, often a modest daily rate that makes them the most affordable option in the city by far. The trade-off is competition: places are limited, and popular subsidised crèches in central communes can have waitlists running well over a year.
- **Private crèches and daycares** charge a flat monthly or daily rate regardless of income, usually several times higher than the subsidised rate, but with shorter waitlists and, in some cases, more flexible hours or bilingual/international programming aimed at expat families.

There's also a middle tier of **independent registered childminders** (*accueillantes* / *onthaalouders*), who care for a small number of children in their own home. Fees sit between the two extremes, and the smaller group size suits some babies and toddlers better than a larger crèche setting.

## When to start looking: earlier than you think

The single most common regret among parents here is not registering soon enough. In Brussels, it's entirely normal, and often necessary, to put your name down for subsidised crèche places while still pregnant, sometimes as early as the first trimester. Popular crèches in central and EU-quarter-adjacent communes fill their intake for an entire year within weeks of opening registration.

A practical approach:

1. **Register with several options at once.** Apply to multiple subsidised crèches, at least one private option, and a registered childminder in parallel. Belgian parents rarely rely on a single application, and neither should you.
2. **Use the official search tools** from ONE and Kind en Gezin to find registered providers near your home or workplace, and confirm current openings, fees, and registration windows directly with each crèche, availability shifts constantly and changes commune by commune.
3. **Have a bridge plan.** Parental leave in Belgium is generous by international standards but still finite. If your subsidised spot doesn't come through in time, a private crèche, a childminder, or a temporary nanny-share with another family can cover the gap.

## Where availability tends to be better

Central, densely populated communes (central Brussels, Ixelles, Saint-Gilles, Schaerbeek) tend to have the longest waitlists relative to supply, simply because demand is highest there. Families sometimes find more breathing room, and larger private and international daycare options, in communes like Uccle, Woluwe-Saint-Lambert, or Etterbeek. ## Costs to budget for

For subsidised crèches, expect a daily fee calculated from household income, often modest for lower and middle incomes and rising for higher earners, but almost always cheaper than private care. Private crèches and international daycares charge flat monthly fees that vary by neighbourhood and hours of care, and it's worth asking upfront about registration deposits, holiday closures (many crèches close for a fixed period in summer), and whether meals and nappies are included or billed separately.

Whichever route you take, factor childcare into your monthly budget early rather than assuming it will slot in around whatever's left, for many dual-income families in Brussels it's one of the largest recurring costs after rent.

## A note for coliving and shared households

Most coliving in Brussels is designed around individuals and couples rather than families with young children, so if you're arriving with a baby or toddler you'll likely be looking at a standard rental rather than a coliving room. That said, the wider community around Brussels' [coliving operators](/actors) is often a surprisingly good source of word-of-mouth recommendations, plenty of current and former residents are parents themselves, and informal referrals to a trusted childminder or a crèche with a sudden opening can move faster than any waitlist.

## Getting registered with the system first

Before you can enrol in most subsidised childcare, you'll typically need to be properly registered as a resident, since many crèches ask for proof of commune registration as part of the application. ## The bottom line

Brussels childcare rewards early planning far more than it punishes indecision. Start researching and registering as soon as you know a move or a birth is coming, apply broadly rather than betting on one crèche, and treat the language-network split as a detail to navigate rather than a barrier. Do that, and you'll likely land in one of the reasons families end up loving raising kids in this city in the first place: it's compact, green, and full of options once you know where to look.
