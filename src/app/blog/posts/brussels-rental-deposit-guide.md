# How to Get Your Full Rental Deposit Back in Brussels

A rental deposit in Brussels is rarely small. Between a traditional landlord and a coliving operator, you're typically looking at one to three months' rent sitting somewhere you can't touch it for the length of your stay. Most of that money comes back, but the difference between getting it all and losing a chunk of it almost always comes down to paperwork you handled (or didn't) on day one, not anything dramatic that happens on your way out. Here's how the system actually works, and how to protect yourself at both ends of a stay.

## How deposits work in Brussels

Belgian rental law treats the deposit as the tenant's money the whole time, held as a guarantee rather than handed over to the landlord. For a classic lease, that usually means one of a few set-ups:

- **A blocked bank account (compte bloqué / geblokkeerde rekening)** opened in your name, where the deposit sits and earns a small amount of interest until both parties sign off on its release. This is the standard route and the reason a Belgian bank account matters so early in a move, see our [guide to opening one](/blog/how-to-open-bank-account-brussels) if you haven't yet.
- **A bank guarantee**, where a bank fronts the deposit on your behalf for a fee, useful if you don't want to lock up cash.
- **A guarantee fund**, run by the Brussels region for tenants who qualify, letting you pay the deposit in instalments rather than as a lump sum.

Coliving operators tend to simplify this considerably. Many hold a smaller deposit (often one to two months) directly rather than through a blocked account, faster to set up, but worth confirming in writing exactly how and when it gets returned, a question worth adding to your list if you're still comparing [coliving operators](/actors) or deciding what to [ask before signing](/blog/coliving-brussels-what-to-ask-before-signing).

Whichever route you're on, the amount is usually fixed at signing and shouldn't move during your stay.

## The document that decides everything: état des lieux

If there's one piece of paperwork that determines whether you get your deposit back, it's the **état des lieux** (NL: *plaatsbeschrijving*), the inventory report describing the state of the property. You'll typically do two:

1. **État des lieux d'entrée** — completed when you move in, documenting every mark, scuff, and existing issue.
2. **État des lieux de sortie** — completed when you move out, compared directly against the first one.

Any difference between the two that isn't explained by normal wear becomes the basis for a deduction. This is why the move-in version matters so much more than people expect, it's the baseline everything else gets measured against.

**Do this on move-in day, every time:**

- Walk every room slowly and note anything imperfect, however minor, scuffed paint, a worn hinge, a stained grout line, a loose cabinet handle.
- Take dated photos and a short video of every room, including close-ups of anything already damaged, and keep them somewhere you'll still have access to in a year or three.
- If a professional inventory clerk does the report, read it before signing and query anything it missed. If it's informal (common in some coliving set-ups), write your own list and email it to the landlord or operator the same day, so there's a dated record even without a formal document.

This single habit, thorough photos plus a written inventory on day one, resolves most deposit disputes before they start.

## Normal wear and tear vs. damage

Belgian tenancy rules distinguish between ordinary use of a property and damage, and only the second gives grounds for a deduction.

- **Normal wear (not deductible):** faded paint from sunlight, worn carpet in a high-traffic hallway, small nail holes from hanging pictures.
- **Damage (potentially deductible):** a cigarette burn, a broken window, a stained carpet from an uncleaned spill, missing items that were present at move-in.

The line isn't always obvious, which is exactly why the two inventory reports matter: if something was already there at move-in, it can't be charged to you at move-out, however bad it looks.

## What can legitimately be deducted

Beyond genuine damage, a landlord or operator can usually deduct for:

- **Cleaning**, if the property isn't left in a reasonably (not spotlessly) clean state.
- **Unpaid bills**, such as a final utility invoice arriving after you've left, if you set those up directly.
- **Missing inventory**, furniture or items listed on move-in that aren't there at move-out.
- **Unfulfilled notice**, if you leave without giving the agreed notice period, covered in our [renting in Brussels guide](/blog/renting-in-brussels-guide).

What shouldn't be deducted: general "refurbishment" of a property that was already dated at move-in, or wear the move-in inventory already shows as pre-existing.

## The move-out process, step by step

1. **Give proper notice.** Check your contract's notice period (commonly one to three months for coliving, longer for traditional leases) and send it in writing, with some form of dated confirmation.
2. **Clean before the final walkthrough.** You don't need it spotless, but a basic clean removes the most common, easiest-to-dispute deduction.
3. **Do the état des lieux de sortie together if possible.** Walk through with the landlord, agent, or house manager rather than relying on a report you only see after the fact, and take your own photos too.
4. **Settle final utilities and bills** if you were paying any directly.
5. **Confirm the return timeline in writing.** A blocked bank account requires both parties' sign-off, which can take a few weeks if either side is slow. For a coliving deposit, ask for a specific date rather than "soon."

## If there's a dispute

If a deduction seems unfair, raise it in writing immediately, referencing your move-in inventory and photos. Most disagreements resolve at this stage once there's clear dated evidence on the table. If it doesn't, Brussels tenants can turn to the **justice of the peace (juge de paix / vredegerechter)** for their commune, which handles rental disputes without needing a lawyer, or seek free guidance from a tenants' rights organisation first. Keep every email and document; the paper trail is almost always what settles these cases.

## Why this matters more in a coliving context

One underrated advantage of coliving is that the people managing your move-out usually handle dozens a year and run a standard process, which tends to make disputes less common than with an individual landlord handling it occasionally. But it cuts both ways: a lighter, less formal deposit process is faster when it works in your favour and leaves you less paper trail when it doesn't. If you're still choosing where to live, it's worth weighing this alongside everything else on the [neighbourhood matchmaker](/matchmaker), and if you're mid-search, browsing [coliving operators](/actors) or the [neighbourhood guides](/neighborhoods) with deposit terms specifically in mind.

## A quick checklist

- [ ] Confirm in writing how your deposit is held (blocked account, bank guarantee, or held directly) and the exact amount
- [ ] Complete a detailed état des lieux d'entrée and keep a copy
- [ ] Take dated photos and video of every room on move-in day
- [ ] Note anything pre-existing in writing, even informally, if there's no professional report
- [ ] Give notice in writing, within the required period, before moving out
- [ ] Clean before the final walkthrough
- [ ] Do the état des lieux de sortie in person if at all possible
- [ ] Get a specific date for the deposit's return, not a vague timeframe

## The bottom line

Deposit disputes in Brussels are rarely about genuine disagreement over what happened, they're about missing evidence from move-in day. A thorough inventory and a folder of dated photos cost you twenty minutes when you arrive and save you weeks of back-and-forth when you leave. Treat the état des lieux as the most important document you'll sign in your first week, because for your deposit, it effectively is.
