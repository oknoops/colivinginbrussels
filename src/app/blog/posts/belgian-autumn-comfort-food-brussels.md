# Belgian Autumn Comfort Food: What to Eat in Brussels Right Now

Brussels eats seasonally in a way that catches a lot of newcomers off guard. Walk past the same brasserie in July and October and you'll find a completely different chalkboard outside: the salads and tartares give way to stews, game, and anything that can be served with a jug of beer-based sauce. If you've just arrived in the city, or you moved here in spring and are experiencing your first proper Belgian autumn, this is the menu you want to understand.

We've kept this guide evergreen on purpose — restaurant names and exact opening dates change, so for what's actually on this week, check [Brussels Kitchen](https://www.brusselskitchen.com) or [visit.brussels](https://www.visit.brussels) rather than relying on a list that will be out of date by next year.

## Game season (la chasse / het jachtseizoen)

From roughly September through December, "gibier" (game) appears on nearly every traditional Belgian and French-influenced menu in the city. This is the season for:

- **Venison (chevreuil)** — usually in a rich sauce with redcurrant or a peppery grand veneur sauce
- **Wild boar (sanglier)** — slow-cooked, often with chestnuts or in a stew
- **Hare (lièvre à la royale)** — a classic, labour-intensive dish that traditional brasseries are proud to put on a seasonal menu
- **Pheasant and other game birds**, less common but worth ordering if you see them

This is a great season to try a classic Brussels brasserie if you haven't yet — look for "menu de chasse" or "menu gibier" signs in the window around the EU Quarter, Sainte-Catherine, and the Grand Place area. It's also one of the more approachable ways to try genuinely traditional Belgian cooking rather than the waffle-and-fries version most tourists see.

## Mushroom season

Cèpes (porcini), girolles (chanterelles), and trompettes de la mort show up at markets and on menus from September into November. They're often served simply — sautéed in butter and parsley as a side — or folded into cream sauces with veal or chicken. If you cook at home, the farmers' stalls at weekend markets are worth a look; our [guide to Brussels' best markets](/blog/brussels-markets-guide) covers where to find them, including the Châtelain and Flagey markets.

## Mussels don't stop in summer

A lot of newcomers assume "moules" are a summer dish because of outdoor terrace season, but mussel season in Belgium actually runs from late summer through winter, and many Belgians consider autumn and early winter the better time to eat them — the mussels are plumper and the weather finally matches the dish (a steaming pot of moules-frites is objectively a cold-weather food). If you haven't tried the classic combinations yet — moules marinière, à la crème, or au vin blanc — now is the moment.

## Stoofvlees, carbonade, and the return of the stew

The dish that defines Belgian comfort food, carbonade flamande (stoofvlees in Dutch), is a beef stew braised in dark Belgian beer, usually with onions and a slice of bread with mustard cooked into the sauce to thicken it. It's on nearly every "plat du jour" board from October onward, usually served with fries or mash. If you want the fuller picture of what to order and when, our [guide to Belgian foods you have to try](/blog/belgian-food-you-must-try) covers the rest of the essentials, from stoemp to waterzooi.

## Street food changes too: roasted chestnuts

Once the temperature drops properly, vendors with small roasting carts start appearing around the busier pedestrian streets and Christmas market areas, selling warm roasted chestnuts (marrons chauds) in paper cones. It's one of the simplest, cheapest seasonal pleasures in the city — a few euros for something warm to hold while you walk.

## Where to actually find all this

You don't need a guidebook of specific restaurant names, because the good news is that seasonal menus are everywhere, not hidden in one or two spots:

- **Traditional brasseries**, especially around the EU Quarter, Sainte-Catherine (the old fish market area), and the streets just off Grand Place
- **Neighbourhood bistros** in Ixelles, Saint-Gilles, and Etterbeek, which often do a better, less touristy version of the same seasonal dishes at a fraction of the price
- **Weekend markets**, if you'd rather cook it yourself
- **Beer bars**, many of which pair seasonal beers (bokbier, winter ales) with heartier food once the terraces close

## A note for coliving households

If you live in a coliving house, autumn is genuinely one of the best times to lean into shared cooking. A pot of carbonade or a mushroom risotto is easy to scale up for a house of six to ten people, it's cheap per portion, and it's the kind of meal that tends to turn into a proper house dinner rather than everyone eating separately in their rooms. It's also a low-effort way to introduce housemates who've just arrived to Belgian food culture without anyone having to cook anything complicated.

## The bottom line

Belgian food doesn't really hit its stride until the weather turns. If you spent your first few months here eating waffles and frites and wondering what the fuss was about, autumn is when the city's food scene actually shows you what it's capable of — heavier, slower, beer-braised, and built for sharing. Go find a brasserie with a chalkboard menu, order whatever involves "sauce" and "gibier," and you'll understand what Belgians mean when they say the cooking here is more serious than it gets credit for.
