# A Newcomer's Guide to Belgian Beer (Beyond Stella)

Belgian beer culture is officially recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage — the only beer culture with that honour. There are well over a thousand Belgian beers, each with its own branded glass, and getting the wrong glass is a minor scandal. It's intimidating at first. This guide gets you from "a Stella, please" to ordering like a local.

## The big families, decoded

### Trappist & Abbey
Brewed by (or under licence from) monasteries. Strong, complex, malty. Belgium has several authentic **Trappist** breweries — **Westmalle**, **Chimay**, **Orval**, **Rochefort**, **Achel**, and the almost-mythical **Westvleteren 12**, routinely called one of the best beers in the world and sold only at the abbey. **Abbey beers** (like Leffe, Grimbergen) are the commercial cousins — good, widely available, less holy.

### Blonde, Dubbel, Tripel, Quadrupel
Rough guide to strength and colour:
*   **Blonde** — golden, drinkable, often deceptively strong (6–7%).
*   **Dubbel** — dark, malty, notes of dried fruit.
*   **Tripel** — pale but powerful (8–9%), the classic being **Westmalle Tripel**.
*   **Quadrupel** — the heavyweight, rich and warming.

A word of warning to newcomers: Belgian beers are **strong**. A "small" tripel can be 9%. Pace yourself — the *après-midi* that starts with "just one" is a rite of passage that ends in regret.

### Lambic, Gueuze & Kriek — the Brussels speciality
This is the one to care about, because it's *local*. **Lambic** is spontaneously fermented — brewed only in the Brussels/Payottenland area, using wild yeast from the air. It's sour, funky, still (uncarbonated), and unlike anything else in the world.
*   **Gueuze** — a blend of young and old lambics, refermented in the bottle. Dry, tart, champagne-like. An acquired taste that becomes an obsession.
*   **Kriek** — lambic aged with cherries. Traditional versions are sour and serious (not the sweet supermarket ones).
*   **Faro** — a sweeter, lightly sugared lambic.

The temple of this tradition is **Cantillon**, a working family brewery in Anderlecht that's also a museum (the *Musée Bruxellois de la Gueuze*). Touring it, then tasting a gueuze at the source, is one of the most authentic things you can do in Brussels.

### Witbier (Blanche)
Cloudy wheat beer with coriander and orange peel — **Hoegaarden** is the original from the Belgian village of the same name. Light, refreshing, the summer terrace default.

## How to order without embarrassing yourself

*   **"Une pils" / "een pintje"** gets you a standard lager (Jupiler or Maes, the everyday Belgian beers). Perfectly respectable.
*   Ask the bartender **"Qu'est-ce que vous avez à la pression?"** (what's on tap) and try the local thing.
*   Each beer comes in **its own glass** — chalice, flute, tulip. Don't ask why; just accept the ritual. If they're out of the right glass, they may genuinely apologise.
*   Belgians drink beer **slowly and socially**. It's about the conversation and the terrace, not the quantity. Sipping a strong tripel over an hour is normal.

## Where to drink it

*   **Delirium Café** (near the centre) — famous for its record-breaking beer menu; fun but touristy. Good for a first "wow."
*   **À la Mort Subite** and **Le Cirio** — gorgeous historic *estaminets* serving lambics in the setting they were meant for.
*   **Moeder Lambic** ([Saint-Gilles](/neighborhoods/saint-gilles) and Fontainas) — beloved by locals and beer nerds, deep and well-curated list.
*   **Cantillon** (Anderlecht) — the pilgrimage. Brewery, museum, and tasting in one.
*   **Any neighbourhood terrace** in spring — honestly the best "where," because the point is the company.

## Beer and food

Belgians cook *with* beer, not just drink it: **carbonnade flamande** (beef braised in brown ale), *lapin à la gueuze* (rabbit in lambic), and cheeses washed in Trappist ale. Pairing a Chimay with a chunk of its own abbey cheese is a small, perfect Belgian afternoon.

## A gentle newcomer warning

Two things every newcomer learns the hard way:
1.  Belgian beer is far stronger than the lager you're used to. Read the ABV.
2.  "Let's grab one beer" is rarely one beer. The Belgian apéro has a gravitational pull.

The upside: the terrace apéro is *the* social engine of Brussels. Saying yes to it — responsibly — is how you meet people. In a [coliving house](/actors), Friday-evening house drinks are a fixture, and there's always someone who knows exactly which gueuze you should try next.

## Your first-month tasting list

Work through these and you'll understand Belgian beer better than most tourists ever do: a **Westmalle Tripel**, an **Orval**, a **Cantillon Gueuze**, a traditional **Kriek**, and a humble **Jupiler** on a terrace with new friends. Santé — *op uw gezondheid!*
