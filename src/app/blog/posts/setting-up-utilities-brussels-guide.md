# Setting Up Utilities in Brussels: Electricity, Gas, Water & Internet

If you've just signed a lease after the September rentrée rush, there's a decent chance your new apartment came with keys, a mailbox, and absolutely nothing else switched on. Unlike many countries where utilities are either included in rent or handled by a single municipal provider, Belgium runs a fully liberalized energy market: you, personally, have to choose a supplier, sign a contract, and get the meters registered in your name. It's one of those admin tasks that's genuinely confusing the first time and completely routine every time after that.

Here's how it breaks down, and the order that actually makes sense.

## Electricity and Gas: You Choose the Supplier

In Brussels, the pipes and wires belong to the distribution network operator, **Sibelga**, but Sibelga doesn't sell you energy. That's done by competing commercial suppliers, and picking one is entirely on you. The main players most newcomers end up comparing are:

- **Engie**, one of the largest and most established, often the default for people who want to not think about it twice
- **Luminus**, the other major incumbent, similarly broad coverage
- **TotalEnergies**, competitive pricing, increasingly common with renters
- **Mega** and **Octa+**, smaller challengers that regularly undercut the big two on price
- **Bolt**, a newer entrant worth checking if you want a fully digital sign-up

Prices and contract terms shift often enough that it's worth comparing rather than defaulting to whichever name you recognize. The Brussels energy regulator **Brugel** runs a free, independent comparison simulator that lets you plug in your estimated consumption and see real quotes side by side, it's the single most useful fifteen minutes you'll spend on this.

To actually sign up, you'll need your address, your **EAN code** (an 18-digit number identifying your specific meter, usually findable on a previous bill, on the meter itself, or by asking Sibelga directly), and a **meter reading** taken on your move-in date. That reading matters: if you don't record it yourself when you arrive, you can end up partly billed for the previous tenant's usage, or vice versa. Take a photo of both the gas and electricity meters the day you get your keys, before you touch anything else.

Most coliving operators and some furnished rentals handle all of this for you and simply fold a flat utilities fee into your monthly rent, which is one of the more underrated conveniences of going that route if admin isn't your idea of a good first week. If you're weighing that trade-off, our guide to [coliving versus renting solo in Brussels](/blog/coliving-vs-flatshare-brussels) walks through what's typically included on each side.

## Water: No Choice Involved

Water is the one utility where Brussels doesn't make you shop around. Distribution runs through **Vivaqua**, with billing handled by **Hydrobru** (or directly in some communes), and the contract is tied to the address rather than to a supplier you select. In practice, this means you usually just need to notify Hydrobru that you've moved in, provide your meter reading, and you're set. If you're in an apartment building rather than a house, water is sometimes billed collectively through the building's co-ownership charges instead, so check your lease before assuming you need to open an account yourself.

## Internet, TV, and Mobile

This is the utility most likely to create a gap you actually feel, because unlike electricity, you can live without broadband for a day or two, you just won't want to. The main fixed-line providers in Brussels are:

- **Proximus**, the former national operator, with the widest coverage and generally the most reliable fibre/copper rollout
- **VOO** (and its budget brand), strong in parts of Brussels, often competitively priced for bundles
- **Orange** and **Scarlet** (Proximus's budget label), both solid no-frills options
- **Telenet**, more Flanders-focused but available in parts of the city

The trap newcomers fall into is assuming installation is instant. It isn't. A technician visit to activate your line can take one to three weeks depending on the provider and whether the building already has active infrastructure, so this is worth booking the moment you have a signed lease and an address, not after you've already moved your boxes in. If your coliving house or building already has internet running, confirm whether you're joining an existing contract or need your own account, shared coliving internet is common and usually just requires a quick request to the house manager.

For mobile, Belgium's three network operators (**Proximus**, **Orange**, **Base**) all have prepaid SIM options you can activate same-day at a shop with just your passport, which is the fastest way to have a working number while the rest of your admin catches up. MVNOs riding on the same networks (Mobile Vikings, Lycamobile, and others) are often cheaper for comparable data.

## What You'll Need to Open Accounts

Across all of these, suppliers will generally ask for the same handful of things, so it's worth having them ready before you start calling or signing up online:

- Your Belgian address and move-in date
- A **Belgian IBAN** for direct debit (most suppliers won't accept a foreign account for recurring billing), so this is a good reason to sort your [Belgian bank account](/blog/how-to-open-bank-account-brussels) early
- Your **national register number**, issued once you register at your commune, some suppliers ask for it, others don't
- The property's EAN code for energy, and a meter reading for both gas/electricity and water

## Getting the Order Right

If you're ticking through the full [move-in checklist](/blog/moving-to-brussels-checklist), utilities sit comfortably in the first week alongside commune registration and your bank account, not before them, since you'll need the IBAN, but not long after either, since estimated bills without an account in your name can get messy to untangle later. A sensible sequence looks like: open your bank account, register at the commune, take your meter readings on move-in day, then sign up for energy and internet within the first few days using the Brugel comparison tool to pick a supplier.

If you're still choosing where to live and want a sense of which buildings tend to have fibre already run or utilities bundled into rent, our [neighbourhood guides](/neighborhoods) and the [coliving matchmaker](/matchmaker) are both good starting points, and several established [coliving operators](/actors) list exactly what's included before you even sign. Either way, this is a one-time setup: get it right in the first week, and it's one less thing you'll ever think about again.
