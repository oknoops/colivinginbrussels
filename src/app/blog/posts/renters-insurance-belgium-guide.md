# Do You Need Renters Insurance in Belgium? A Coliving Resident's Guide to Assurance Habitation

Somewhere between opening a Belgian bank account and figuring out how the recycling bins work, most newcomers hit a line in their rental contract that stops them cold: proof of "assurance habitation" (home insurance) or "brandverzekering" (fire insurance), required before you get the keys. It sounds bureaucratic and faintly alarming, as if the landlord expects a fire. In reality it's one of the cheapest, most routine parts of settling in Brussels, and worth understanding properly rather than just signing up for whatever a broker offers first.

## What this insurance actually covers

Belgian rental insurance bundles a few distinct things under one policy, and they matter in a different order than you'd guess:

- **Civil liability toward third parties** (*responsabilité civile locative / burgerlijke aansprakelijkheid*): this is the part landlords actually care about. If a fire, flood, or burst pipe in your flat damages the building or a neighbour's property, this covers the cost. It's the core of why landlords require proof before handing over keys.
- **Fire, water damage, storm, and glass breakage** to the property itself, protecting the structure and the landlord's fixtures.
- **Theft and contents cover** for your own belongings, laptop, bike, furniture, clothes, which is genuinely optional in most policies and often sold as an add-on.
- **Legal assistance** in some packages, covering disputes with the landlord or neighbours.

The liability piece is the one you can't really skip. The contents piece is the one worth thinking about separately, especially if you've brought anything expensive with you.

## Is it actually required?

There's no single nationwide law forcing every tenant to buy a named policy, but in practice it functions as a requirement almost everywhere in Brussels: most standard lease contracts include a clause making proof of insurance a condition of the tenancy, and landlords can reasonably ask for it before or shortly after move-in. Treat "is it legally mandatory" as the wrong question. The right question is whether your contract requires it (almost always yes, written in black and white) and whether you'd want the liability protection even if it didn't (also yes, for the price involved).

## If you're renting a room or flat directly

If you've signed a standard lease, independent of any [coliving operator](/actors), you'll usually need to arrange your own policy before move-in. The process is simple:

1. Get a quote from a Belgian insurer (Ethias, AG, AXA, Belfius, P&V, and others all sell this product) or through a broker, who can compare several insurers for you in one conversation.
2. Decide how much contents cover you want, insurers typically ask you to estimate the value of what you own, which drives the price far more than the liability portion does.
3. Pay the annual premium, often payable monthly, and ask for the certificate of insurance (*attestation d'assurance / verzekeringsattest*) your landlord will want to see.

This is routinely one of the cheapest recurring costs of moving to Belgium, and it's worth comparing two or three quotes rather than accepting the first one, since contents cover levels vary more than the base liability price does.

## If you're living in coliving

This is where it gets genuinely easier, and where it's worth reading your contract closely rather than assuming. Many Brussels coliving operators carry a building-level insurance policy that already covers fire and liability for the whole house, meaning you may not need to arrange anything separately for that part. What's less consistently covered is **your personal belongings**: a house policy protecting the building doesn't necessarily protect your laptop if it's stolen from your room. Before you sign anything, it's worth adding insurance questions to the list in our guide on [what to ask before signing with a coliving operator](/blog/coliving-brussels-what-to-ask-before-signing): specifically, whether building insurance is included in the rent, and whether you need your own contents policy on top.

If your coliving contract doesn't cover contents, a basic personal belongings policy (sometimes framed as part of a broader "family" or "tenant" policy) is still inexpensive and worth adding, especially if you've shipped or brought anything valuable.

## What happens if you skip it

The scenario insurers exist for is rarer than the daily inconvenience of not having proof ready: a kitchen fire, a washing machine leak that travels down to the flat below, a dishwasher hose that fails overnight. Without liability cover, you're personally on the hook for repairing damage to the building and to neighbours' property, which can run into thousands of euros even for a contained incident. It's the kind of risk that's genuinely worth insuring against for the cost of a few coffees a month, even setting the contractual requirement aside entirely.

## Making a claim, briefly

If something does happen:

- Report it to your insurer as soon as possible, most have a window (often within days) for notifying them.
- Photograph the damage before anything is cleaned up or repaired.
- For theft or break-ins, file a police report first (you'll need the report number for the claim).
- Tell your landlord or coliving operator in parallel, since they'll likely need to coordinate on building-related repairs.

## The bottom line

Assurance habitation in Belgium is less a bureaucratic hurdle than a cheap, sensible safety net, and in most lease contracts it's effectively required reading, not optional. If you're renting independently, get a quote and a certificate before move-in. If you're in coliving, check what the operator's building policy already covers and only add what's missing, usually just contents cover for your own things. Either way, it's a half-hour task that's easy to finish early and easy to regret skipping.
