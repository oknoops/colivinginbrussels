# Moving to Brussels as an American: A US Citizen's Guide

For a **US citizen living in Brussels**, the move is exciting but comes with a learning curve: different paperwork, a smaller-scale city, and a few cultural surprises. This guide walks American newcomers through the essentials — residence basics, housing, banking, healthcare and taxes — so your first months feel a lot less daunting.

> Immigration and tax rules change and depend on your personal situation. Treat this as an orientation, not legal advice, and always confirm the current requirements on official sources (see below).

## Residence and visa basics

As an American, you can enter Belgium visa-free as a tourist for short stays. But if you're **moving here to live, work or study**, you'll generally need the right visa or residence permit arranged before or shortly after arrival, and you'll need to register.

The key points to check with official sources:

- The **type of permit** you need (work, study, family, self-employed) depends on your reason for moving. Verify on the Belgian immigration office and your nearest Belgian consulate's official websites.
- Once you settle, you must **register at your local commune** (domiciliation) — this is required if you stay longer than three months. Your commune's official website lists the exact documents.
- Keep certified copies of your birth certificate, diplomas and other US documents; Belgian admin loves paperwork.

Because rules differ by nationality and situation, don't rely on forum posts — confirm everything on the commune and federal immigration websites.

## Cultural differences Americans notice

Brussels rewards a bit of cultural adjustment:

- **Smaller and slower.** The city is compact and walkable. Shops keep shorter hours and many close on Sundays.
- **Language.** Brussels is officially bilingual (French and Dutch), and very international. English is widely understood, but a few French phrases go a long way with admin and neighbours.
- **Direct but polite.** A greeting ("bonjour") before asking for something is expected in shops and offices.
- **Cash-light, card-heavy.** Bancontact/cards dominate; carry a little cash for markets.

## Finding housing without a Belgian guarantor

This is where many Americans get stuck. Traditional Brussels landlords often want a long lease, a local guarantor and a hefty deposit — tough when you've just arrived with no Belgian credit history.

**Coliving solves this.** Furnished rooms, bills included, flexible 3–6 month leases, and usually **no Belgian guarantor required**. All-in prices generally run €500–€1,500 per month. It's the fastest way to land softly, meet people and figure out which area you like before committing long term.

## Banking, healthcare and taxes

**Banking.** You'll want a Belgian or EU account for rent and daily life. Most banks require proof of address and your registration — another reason coliving (with a real address) speeds things up. Digital banks can be a quick stopgap.

**Healthcare.** Belgium has a strong healthcare system built around mandatory health insurance funds (mutuelles/ziekenfonds). Once you're registered and working, you enrol with one. Keep private travel/health cover for the transition period.

**Taxes — the American catch.** This is the big one: the US taxes citizens on worldwide income no matter where they live, so you'll likely have **both US and Belgian filing obligations**. Belgium and the US have a tax treaty, and mechanisms exist to avoid double taxation, but the details are genuinely complex. Consult a cross-border tax professional and check the official IRS and Belgian tax authority (SPF Finances) websites.

## FAQ

**Can I sign a coliving contract before I have Belgian residency?**
Often yes — many coliving operators accept international tenants and don't require a Belgian guarantor. Confirm each operator's specific requirements when you enquire.

**Do I need to speak French to live in Brussels as an American?**
No, you can get by in English in most expat contexts, but basic French helps enormously with commune paperwork and daily errands.

**How soon do I need to register at the commune?**
Registration (domiciliation) is required if you're staying longer than three months. Check your commune's official website for the exact timeline and documents.
