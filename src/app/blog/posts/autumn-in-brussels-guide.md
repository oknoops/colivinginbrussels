# Autumn in Brussels: The Newcomer's Guide to the City's Best Season

Ask a longtime Brussels resident which season they'd defend to a skeptic, and a surprising number will say autumn. The rentrée rush of September has settled, the summer crowds are gone, and the city moves into a stretch — roughly mid-September through November — that locals quietly consider its most comfortable. The light gets warmer and lower, café terraces trade sun umbrellas for blankets and heat lamps, and the cultural calendar hits full stride. It's also a season with its own logistics: shorter days, two public holidays clustered in November, and a genuine shift in what the city expects you to wear and eat. Here's how to navigate it well.

## What Actually Changes: Weather and Light

Brussels autumns are milder than their reputation suggests, but they are wetter and greyer than most newcomers expect. September and early October often still deliver warm, sunny stretches — arguably the best weather of the year, since summer's humidity has broken. By late October, temperatures settle into the low-to-mid teens (Celsius) during the day, dropping toward single digits at night, and rain becomes a near-daily possibility rather than an occasional event.

The bigger adjustment for most newcomers isn't the temperature but the light. Clocks go back an hour in late October (the shift to standard time), and by November, darkness falls before 5pm. Combined with Brussels' cloud cover, this is the point in the year when a lack of daylight genuinely affects mood for a lot of people. A few practical responses that locals swear by: a daylight lamp for your desk if you work from home, prioritising a room with real windows when you're choosing housing, and treating a midday walk as a habit rather than a luxury while the sun is up.

## The Wardrobe Shift

Layering is the entire strategy. Belgian buildings are generally well-heated indoors, trains and trams run warm, and outdoor conditions swing from a crisp 8°C morning to a drizzly 14°C afternoon within the same day. A good waterproof (not just water-resistant) jacket earns its keep more in Brussels than in almost any other European capital — the rain here tends to be persistent and fine rather than dramatic, and umbrellas lose a lot of battles against the wind. If you're arriving from a drier climate, this is worth budgeting for early rather than improvising with an umbrella you'll replace twice.

## Two Public Holidays You Need to Plan Around

November brings two public holidays in quick succession that catch a lot of newcomers off guard:

- **1 November — Toussaint (All Saints' Day).** Shops, banks, and administrative offices close. Many Belgians use the long weekend to visit family or travel, so if you're planning your own trip, book transport early — flights and trains around this date fill up similarly to a long holiday weekend anywhere else in Europe.
- **11 November — Armistice Day.** A public holiday marking the end of the First World War, with a ceremony at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. Offices and most shops close again.

Because these two holidays land less than two weeks apart, it's common for Brussels' rhythm to feel start-stop through November — plan admin errands, deliveries, and appointments around both dates rather than assuming a normal working month.

## What's Actually Good About This Season

**The cultural calendar peaks.** Theatres, concert halls, and museums launch their new season's programming in September and run it through the winter, so autumn is genuinely one of the best times of year to catch new exhibitions and performances before December's holiday crowds arrive. If you haven't already, our [festivals and events calendar](/blog/brussels-festivals-events-calendar) is worth checking for what's on.

**Comfort food season begins in earnest.** Belgian cuisine leans hard into autumn and winter — game meats, stews braised in beer, mushroom dishes, and the first *speculoos*-flavoured everything appearing in bakery windows. It's a good excuse to work through the dishes on our guide to [Belgian food you must try](/blog/belgian-food-you-must-try) if you haven't yet.

**Parks put on a real show.** The Bois de la Cambre, Parc du Cinquantenaire, and Forêt de Soignes all turn color in October, and with tourist numbers down, they're noticeably quieter than in summer. See our guide to [Brussels' parks and green spaces](/blog/brussels-parks-green-spaces-guide) for the best routes.

**Housing and social life settle down.** The September scramble for leases and registration appointments has passed, viewing competition eases, and the social groups, language exchanges, and clubs that filled up in September are now running at a steady, easier-to-join pace.

## Settling In for the Season, Not Just Passing Through

If you moved to Brussels over the summer or during the September rentrée, autumn is typically the first real test of whether your setup works for the long haul — the first stretch of genuinely bad weather, shorter days, and more time spent indoors. A few things worth checking now rather than in the middle of a cold snap:

- Does your room or apartment get natural light, and is the heating reliable? This matters far more from November onward than it did in July.
- Is your commute manageable in the rain and dark, or would a different neighbourhood cut real time off it? The [neighbourhood matchmaker](/matchmaker) is a fast way to re-check this against your actual priorities.
- If you're in a shared house or coliving space, autumn and winter are when a well-run common area and reliable heating stop being nice-to-haves and start mattering daily. Browsing [coliving operators](/actors) or the [neighbourhood guides](/neighborhoods) is worth doing now if your current setup isn't holding up.

## An Autumn Checklist

- [ ] Buy a proper waterproof jacket, not just an umbrella
- [ ] Get a daylight lamp if you work from a room with limited natural light
- [ ] Mark 1 and 11 November on your calendar and plan admin/travel around them
- [ ] Check your new season's cultural programming while tickets are still easy to get
- [ ] Test your commute in the rain and dark before you're relying on it daily
- [ ] Confirm your heating situation before temperatures drop further

## The Bottom Line

Autumn is when Brussels stops performing for tourists and starts being genuinely pleasant to live in — golden parks, a full cultural calendar, and a slower pace, offset by shorter days and two holidays that briefly scramble the month of November. Newcomers who treat the season shift as something to plan for, rather than something that just happens to them, tend to enjoy this stretch more than almost any other time of year in the city.
