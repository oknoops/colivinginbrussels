# How to Fit In in Brussels: A Newcomer's Playbook

Brussels is one of the easiest cities in the world to *arrive* in — and one of the trickiest to actually feel *part of*. A third of the population is foreign, the expat bubble is comfortable, and you can go months only speaking English to other newcomers. Fitting in takes a little intention. Here's the playbook.

## 1. Escape the bubble (a little)

The EU-and-NATO expat scene is warm, easy, and self-contained. It's also a trap if your goal is to feel rooted. You don't have to abandon it — just puncture it:

*   Join one thing that isn't international by design: a neighbourhood sports club, a Belgian choir, a local *asbl* (non-profit), a pottery class in your commune.
*   Shop at the same *boulangerie*, greengrocer, and café until they know your face. Belgians are slow to warm but neighbourhood regulars get adopted.
*   Follow your commune's page for local events. Brussels life is intensely local — your commune (Ixelles, Saint-Gilles, Schaerbeek…) is almost a small town of its own.

## 2. Learn the language of hello

You don't need fluent French. You need **the polite framing**. Always open with "Bonjour" (or "Goedendag") before you ask for anything — in shops, at the counter, on the phone. Skipping the greeting is the single most common way newcomers accidentally seem rude. Add "s'il vous plaît," "merci," "bonne journée," and you've covered 80% of daily goodwill for the price of four words.

From there, a beginner French course pays off socially far more than professionally. See our take on [French or Dutch](/blog/french-or-dutch-brussels-language).

## 3. Master the rhythms of the week

Brussels runs on rhythms newcomers often miss:

*   **Sunday markets** are social institutions, not just shopping. Gare du Midi (huge, cheap, chaotic), Flagey, Châtelain on Wednesdays. Going regularly plugs you into a neighbourhood's pulse. (More in our [markets guide](/blog/brussels-markets-guide).)
*   **Apéro** culture is real. The after-work drink on a terrace — a *pils*, a *spritz*, a plate of cheese and salami — is where friendships actually form. Say yes to apéro.
*   **Sundays are slow.** Many shops close. Lean into it: a long lunch, a walk in the Bois de la Cambre, a museum. Our [perfect Sunday guide](/blog/perfect-sunday-in-brussels) has a full plan.

## 4. Embrace the weather philosophy

It rains. A lot. Fighting it makes you miserable; adopting the Belgian attitude makes you resilient. Belgians don't cancel plans for grey skies — they put on a coat and go anyway, then celebrate disproportionately when the sun appears (the first warm day empties every office into every terrace in the city). Buy good shoes, a real raincoat, and stop checking the forecast with hope. *Amai, it's raining again* — say it with a shrug and you're halfway Belgian.

## 5. Get your admin done — it's a rite of passage

Nothing makes you feel like a resident faster than surviving Belgian bureaucracy. Registering at your commune (domiciliation), getting your residence card, sorting your *mutuelle* (health insurance) — it's tedious, occasionally absurd, and universal. Every Belgian and every expat has war stories. Getting through it is a bonding topic and a genuine milestone. Our [registration guide](/blog/how-to-register-brussels-expat-guide) walks you through it.

Tip: a [coliving address](/coliving-brussels) usually allows domiciliation, which removes one of the biggest headaches newcomers face.

## 6. Eat like a local, not like a tourist

Fitting in has a delicious shortcut: food. Skip the Grand-Place tourist traps and eat where locals eat — a *mitraillette* from a *fritkot*, a *stoemp* in a brown café, fresh croquettes at a fish bar, a *cuberdon* from a market stall. Knowing your *carbonnade* from your *waterzooi* is cultural currency. Start with our list of [13 Belgian foods to try](/blog/belgian-food-you-must-try) and [where locals actually eat](/blog/where-locals-eat-brussels).

## 7. Find your people through shared living

The honest truth: the newcomers who feel at home fastest almost always have one thing in common — they didn't arrive into an empty studio. Whether it's a flatshare or a managed [coliving space](/actors), living with others gives you an instant micro-community, built-in local translators, and a social calendar you didn't have to build alone. Weekly house dinners do more for integration than any app.

If you're weighing your options, our [coliving vs flatshare guide](/blog/coliving-vs-flatshare-brussels) breaks down the trade-offs.

## 8. Say yes for the first six months

The single best strategy: **default to yes** for your first half-year. Yes to the house dinner, the random apéro, the colleague's birthday, the neighbour's *asbl* fundraiser, the Sunday market with someone you barely know. Fitting in is a numbers game — the more rooms you walk into, the faster the city stops feeling foreign.

## The one-line version

Greet before you ask, say yes to apéro, get through the paperwork, love the fries, and don't fight the rain. Do that for six months and Brussels quietly becomes yours.
