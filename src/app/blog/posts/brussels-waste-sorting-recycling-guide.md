# Waste Sorting in Brussels: The Newcomer's Guide to Getting It Right

Of all the small culture shocks that come with moving to Brussels, waste sorting is one of the most universal. It doesn't matter whether you're arriving from a country with almost no sorting requirements or one with an even stricter system than Belgium's — the specific combination of coloured bags, street-by-street collection days, and glass bubbles tucked into random corners takes a few weeks to click. Get it wrong for too long, though, and you risk uncollected bags, annoyed neighbours, or a fine from the commune.

Here's how the system actually works, and the mistakes worth avoiding from week one.

## The Basics: Brussels Sorts by Bag Colour, Not by Bin

Unlike cities where you sort into different coloured wheelie bins kept at your property, most of the Brussels-Capital Region sorts by **coloured bags**, which you buy yourself and put out on the pavement on your street's specific collection day. The main categories are:

- **White bags** — general household waste that doesn't fit any other category (this is the most expensive bag, deliberately, to encourage sorting into the others).
- **Blue bags** — PMC waste: plastic packaging, metal packaging (tins, cans, aluminium foil), and drink cartons (tetra packs).
- **Yellow bags** — paper and cardboard (though small households often use a simple bundled stack or a cardboard box instead of a bag for this one).
- **Orange bags** — food and organic waste, increasingly rolled out across communes as the region expands organic collection.

Only the official bags sold under the Brussels-Capital Region system count — you can't substitute a generic bin liner and expect it to be collected. They're sold in most supermarkets, usually near the till or in the cleaning aisle, in rolls of varying sizes.

## Collection Days Are Street-Specific — Always Check Yours

This is the detail that catches almost everyone out: collection days aren't citywide, and they're not even always the same for every bag colour on your own street. Bags typically need to be out on the pavement the evening before collection (after a certain hour) rather than in the morning, and putting them out too early is itself a minor offence in some communes.

Rather than guessing or asking a neighbour who might be wrong, look up your exact address on Bruxelles-Propreté's official calendar tool, or download the **Recycle!** app, which gives you a personalised schedule and sends reminders the night before. If you've just moved, this is worth doing in your first few days — it's a five-minute task that saves weeks of bags piling up in a hallway. ## Glass Never Goes in a Bag

Glass bottles and jars are the one category that doesn't use the bag system at all. Instead, they go into the public **glass bubbles** (bulles à verre) — the large igloo-shaped containers found on street corners and near parks across the city, usually split into separate openings for clear and coloured glass. Never put glass in your household bags; it's one of the more common mistakes among newcomers and can result in a bag being left uncollected.

A practical tip: keep a small box or tote bag by your door specifically for glass, and drop it off on your way to the shops or the metro rather than letting it accumulate.

## Bulk Waste and Furniture

Getting rid of furniture, mattresses, or large appliances works differently from your weekly bags. You have two main options: book a free at-home collection through Bruxelles-Propreté for bulky items (numbers are limited per year and need to be scheduled in advance), or bring items yourself to a **Recypark** (the region's recycling centres), which also accept things like electronics, batteries, and garden waste that don't belong in any household bag at all.

This is especially relevant if you're furnishing a new place — it's worth knowing where your nearest Recypark is before you're stuck with packaging from a furniture delivery or a mattress you're replacing. ## Sorting in a Shared House or Coliving Space

If you're moving into a flatshare or a coliving house rather than a solo apartment, waste sorting becomes a shared responsibility — and a surprisingly common source of housemate friction. A few things worth sorting out early with whoever you're living with:

- **Agree who buys the bags.** It's a small recurring cost that's easy to forget splitting fairly.
- **Label the bins clearly**, especially in a house with residents from different countries who are used to different systems.
- **Assign or rotate the task of taking bags out**, since missing a collection day means storing smelly waste for another week.

Most established coliving operators in Brussels already have a system in place — clearly marked sorting stations, a shared calendar, or a house manager who handles it — which is one less thing to figure out compared to an unfurnished flatshare. ## What Happens If You Get It Wrong

Communes and Bruxelles-Propreté do check bags, and repeated or obvious violations — general waste in a blue bag, glass or bulky items left beside bags rather than sorted properly — can result in a bag simply being left uncollected with a sticker explaining why, or in a fine for persistent cases. The system relies heavily on residents sorting honestly, and enforcement varies by commune, so it's not worth testing.

The good news is that once you've bought your first rolls of bags, found your collection day, and located your nearest glass bubble, the whole system becomes background habit within a month or two — genuinely one of the easier parts of settling into Brussels once you know where to look.

## The Short Version

- Buy official coloured bags (white, blue, yellow, orange) from supermarkets — generic bags won't be collected.
- Check your street's specific collection days via Bruxelles-Propreté's calendar or the Recycle! app.
- Glass always goes in a public glass bubble, never in a household bag.
- Book a free bulk collection or use a Recypark for furniture and large items.
- In a shared house or coliving space, agree on bag-buying and bin duties early to avoid friction.

Getting the basics right in your first week saves a surprising amount of hassle later — and it's one small piece of the wider settling-in process that's much easier with the right housing setup from the start.
