# Belgian Workplace Culture: A Newcomer's Guide to the Office

Brussels is a strange professional melting pot. Walk into an EU institution and you'll hear six languages before lunch. Walk into a Belgian SME twenty minutes away and the tone, pace and unwritten rules can feel like a different country entirely. If you've just moved here for work, or you're weighing up a job offer alongside [where to live in Brussels](/blog/where-to-live-brussels-neighborhood-comparison), it helps to know which version of "Brussels professional culture" you're actually walking into, because there are really two of them, and most newcomers only hear about one.

## Two office cultures, one city

The **"EU bubble"** (Commission, Parliament, Council, the lobby firms and consultancies orbiting them) runs on an international, often Anglo-Saxon-influenced style: English as the working language, relatively flat hierarchies on the surface, a culture of networking events, and a workforce that's used to colleagues leaving after two years for Geneva or Nairobi. If your job is in or around the European Quarter, this is probably your world, and it will feel familiar if you've worked in an international NGO or consultancy before.

**Belgian companies**, on the other hand, run on older, more local norms: more hierarchy than they let on, more formality in writing than in speech, and a work-life boundary that is taken far more seriously than in many Anglo-Saxon offices. Neither style is "better", but confusing the two is where most newcomers stumble, assuming EU-bubble informality applies in a traditional Belgian firm, or bringing corporate hustle culture into a company that genuinely stops answering emails at 6pm.

## Hierarchy is real, even when it doesn't look like it

Belgian offices rarely perform hierarchy the way some cultures do, there's no shouting, no visible deference, and your director might take the same tram as you. But decisions still flow up and down a fairly clear chain, and skipping levels (going straight to your boss's boss with an idea or complaint) is read as a serious breach of protocol, not initiative. The safer pattern: raise it with your direct manager first, let them decide whether and how it goes further.

Meetings reflect this too. Don't be surprised if a meeting with ten people produces very little open debate, then the real negotiating happens afterwards in one-on-one corridor conversations or over coffee. Silence in the room doesn't mean agreement, it often means the conversation hasn't actually started yet.

## Email is formal, speech is not

This catches a lot of people out. Spoken Belgian workplace culture is often warm, dry-humoured and refreshingly understated (see the national gift for self-deprecating *zwanze* humour), but written communication, especially email, tends to stay noticeably formal even between people who are quite friendly in person. Opening lines like "Bonjour [Name]," or "Beste [Name],", a clear request, and a formal closing ("Bien à vous" / "Met vriendelijke groeten") are the default, even internally. Skipping straight to a casual "Hey!" in a first email to someone senior can land as overly familiar, even if that same person is relaxed and joking with you at the coffee machine an hour later.

## Punctuality, meetings, and the coffee ritual

For anything professional, meetings start on time, and so should you. This is one context where the more relaxed "Belgian quarter-hour" tolerance for social events does **not** apply. Arriving five minutes late to a client meeting is noticed.

What also gets noticed if you skip it: the coffee break. The short, informal gathering around the coffee machine (or, in many Belgian offices, an actual proper espresso machine) is where a surprising amount of real relationship-building and even decision-shaping happens. Treating it as optional or a waste of time is a common expat mistake, it's not slacking, it's part of how Belgian offices actually function.

## Dress code: smart but understated

Belgian professional dress leans towards quietly polished rather than flashy: good-quality basics, neutral colours, well-kept shoes. Overtly branded or statement outfits can read as trying too hard. In the EU bubble, suits are more common at senior and client-facing levels, while in creative or tech sectors, "smart casual" is standard almost everywhere in Brussels. When in doubt for a first week or an interview, dress one notch more formal than you think you'll need, and adjust down once you've seen the room.

## Work-life balance is a genuine cultural value, not a perk

This is where Belgium diverges most sharply from hustle-culture expectations. The standard working week is often 37 or 38 hours with compensation days, annual leave is generous by international standards (typically 20+ statutory days, often more by sector agreement), and answering work messages late at night or over the weekend is unusual outside of genuinely urgent situations. Taking your full holiday allowance isn't a sign of low ambition, it's the norm, and colleagues will often ask why you *haven't* booked your summer break yet rather than questioning why you have.

This extends to lunch: a proper, often sit-down lunch break is common and not something to apologise for or eat at your desk through, especially outside the EU bubble's more international pace.

## Language at work

Brussels is officially bilingual French/Dutch, but English dominates in international companies and is widely used as a working bridge language everywhere else. Still, a few words of French or Dutch, especially a proper greeting in the morning, go a long way with Belgian colleagues and support staff. If you're planning to stay long-term, [learning French in Brussels](/blog/learn-french-in-brussels) (or Dutch, depending on your sector and side of the city) pays off professionally, not just socially, and our guide to [French or Dutch in Brussels](/blog/french-or-dutch-brussels-language) can help you decide which to prioritise.

## Networking the Belgian way

Belgian professional networking tends to happen through recurring, relatively low-key touchpoints rather than big flashy events: sector associations, alumni groups, regular after-work drinks (a *vernissage* or industry *apéro*), and the kind of long-running professional friendships that start slow and deepen over years. The EU bubble runs a parallel, more event-heavy networking circuit of panels, receptions and policy breakfasts. Both are worth tapping into, but they reward different approaches: patience and consistency for the Belgian track, visibility and follow-up for the EU-bubble track.

## Why this matters if you're coliving

If you've moved to Brussels for work and landed in a [coliving house](/actors), your housemates are often your fastest, most honest source of exactly this kind of unwritten cultural intel, several of them have likely already made the same mistakes you're about to make, in the same sectors. ## The short version

Belgian workplace culture rewards patience over speed, formality in writing over formality in speech, and a genuine respect for the boundary between work and the rest of life. Get those three things right, show up on time, write a properly formal email, and actually take your holiday, and you'll find Brussels offices far warmer and more straightforward than the stereotypes suggest. The learning curve is real, but it's short, and most of it just takes one or two slightly awkward months before it clicks.
